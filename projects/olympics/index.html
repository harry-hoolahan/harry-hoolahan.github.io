Economics and the Olympics

What the data says

I first built this in 2021 as a University of Bristol data science project. For this version I audited the data against an independent medal table, corrected four rows, recomputed every number and added a model. The four charts are the original ones.

  1. Economic size is the strongest signal. Holding population and hosting constant, a 10% larger economy goes with about 11% more medals (elasticity 1.10, 95% interval 0.98 to 1.22).
  2. Population does not help once GDP is known. The coefficient on population is negative (−0.37), which says medals follow total economic size rather than headcount. GDP and population are correlated at 0.87, so I would not read that coefficient as more than that.
  3. Hosting is worth roughly double. A host nation wins about 2.2 times the medals its size would predict (95% interval 1.6 to 3.0), from three hosts: China, the United Kingdom and Brazil.
  4. Public spending on sport shows no link in Europe. Across 31 European countries the correlation between spending per person and Rio 2016 medals is −0.06.

1. Does a bigger economy win more medals?

What to notice. Almost nothing below $10 billion of GDP wins a medal, and the cloud rises steadily to the right.

Yes, with a lot of scatter. Across the three Games, 117 country entries had GDP below $10 billion and only five of them won a medal: Samoa and Togo in 2008, Grenada in 2012 and 2016, and Burundi in 2016. Pooling all three Games, the correlation between the log of GDP and medals won is 0.51. It is weakest for African countries (0.41) and strongest for Europe (0.70).

Nigeria has one of the largest economies in Africa and won a single medal at Rio 2016. Kenya, Ethiopia and South Africa won 13, 8 and 10 that year.

I used GDP at purchasing power parity because it compares countries better than market exchange rates. I used a log scale on the x-axis because GDP in 2016 runs from $0.04 billion (Tuvalu) to $19,097 billion (the United States), and a linear axis would squash most countries into the left edge.

2. Does a bigger population win more medals?

What to notice. The shape looks like Figure 1, but the fit is looser. India and China sit at the same population and a long way apart on medals.

Figure 2 looks a lot like Figure 1, and that is not a coincidence. Across these countries the log of GDP and the log of population have a correlation of 0.87, so the two charts are not independent evidence. Population on its own is a weaker guide to medals than GDP: the correlation is 0.40.

A bigger pool of potential athletes is the obvious explanation, but India shows it is not enough. China, with 1,387.79 million people, won 70 medals at Rio 2016. India, with 1,324.52 million, won two.

3. Which countries do best for their size?

What to notice. The top of the list is mostly small countries, many of them islands.

Small Caribbean nations stand out. Grenada ranked first in both 2012 and 2016, with a single medal each time, which works out at roughly 107,000 and 110,000 people per medal. In 2008 the Bahamas ranked first, ahead of Samoa and Jamaica.

One caution: a country with one medal and a tiny population tops this ranking, so the chart rewards a single lucky result. It is better read as a list of overachievers than as a measure of sporting strength.

4. Does public spending on sport buy medals?

What to notice. The two highest spenders are on the floor of the chart.

Not in this sample. The correlation between spending per person and medals won is close to zero (−0.06). I expected richer countries to spend more and win more, and that is not what these 31 countries show.

Luxembourg and Iceland spent €435.45 and €495.43 per person on average and won no medals at Rio 2016. The United Kingdom spent €75.70 per person and won 67.

5. Separating GDP, population and hosting

The charts compare one factor at a time, and GDP and population move together. To separate them I fitted a Poisson regression of medals on log GDP, log population, a host dummy and Games dummies, using robust standard errors. A Poisson model suits counts that cannot be negative and are often zero. I checked the fit against a separate hand-written implementation, and the coefficients agree to six decimal places.

What these results cannot tell you

  • Correlation, not cause. Even with a model, this is observational data. Richer countries may win more for reasons that are not about money.
  • Three Games only, and three hosts. The host effect rests on China, the United Kingdom and Brazil.
  • Figure 4 mixes units. It compares spending per person with total medals, so a small, rich country can spend heavily and still not win many medals simply because it is small.
  • Europe only. The spending data covers 31 European countries, so Figure 4 says nothing about the rest of the world.
  • Some countries are missing. The World Bank has no PPP GDP series for Cuba, North Korea, Chinese Taipei, Venezuela or the Independent Olympic Athletes, so their 84 medals are not in the GDP analysis.

Data and method

  1. CollectWorld Bank GDP and population by wbgapi; medal tables from Wikipedia; Eurostat spending CSV.
  2. JoinOn three-letter ISO codes, because country names differ between sources.
  3. AuditCompared with an independent table built from athlete-level results.
  4. CorrectFour rows fixed and logged in CORRECTIONS.md.
  5. ModelPoisson regression, cross-checked by hand; results saved as JSON.
  6. PublishCharts, tables and explorer read the same files.

GDP (indicator NY.GDP.MKTP.PP.KD) and population (SP.POP.TOTL) came from the World Bank API using the wbgapi Python package in Google Colab. Medal tables were scraped from Wikipedia with BeautifulSoup. Spending data is a CSV download from Eurostat. Joining medals to World Bank data by country name failed in many cases, so I used the World Bank's economy coder to get ISO codes and joined on those, then dropped countries with missing GDP or population.

The data audit

I rebuilt a medal table from the TidyTuesday athlete-level Olympic history file and compared it country by country. That found four clear errors: Germany and France had zero medals in 2012, Brazil had zero in 2016, and Iran was credited with 44 medals in 2012 instead of 12. All four are fixed in both data files and logged in CORRECTIONS.md. The fixes moved the pooled GDP correlation from 0.49 to 0.51 and the European one from 0.65 to 0.70.
